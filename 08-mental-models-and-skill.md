# Part 16 — The Deepest Mental Models

Eighteen models. Each tagged **[Book]** where it's Fitzpatrick's idea restated, **[Synthesis]** where it's my extension. Each ends with a one-sentence rule to remember.

---

### 1. The Mom Test itself
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** Ask questions that even someone who loves you can't answer with a comforting lie.
- **Explanation:** The problem isn't dishonest people. It's questions that make honesty socially expensive. Fix the question and the honesty comes free.
- **Example:** "Would you buy a cookbook app?" vs. "When did you last cook from a recipe, and where did it come from?"
- **Founder application:** Run every question through: about them? about the past? answerable with a fact?
- **Common mistake:** Trying to find "brutally honest" people instead of fixing the question.
- **Rule:** *Fix the question, not the person.*

### 2. Past behavior beats future intention
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** What someone did is evidence. What they say they'll do is mood.
- **Explanation:** People predict their own behavior badly and optimistically. They report their history reasonably well.
- **Example:** "I'd definitely pay for that" vs. "I paid $400 for a tool last year and canceled after two months."
- **Founder application:** Convert every future-tense question to a past-tense one before asking it.
- **Common mistake:** Counting "definitely" as a customer.
- **Rule:** *Ask what they did, never what they'd do.*

### 3. Compliments are not evidence
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** A compliment tells you they're polite and you were pitching. Nothing else.
- **Explanation:** Compliments cost nothing and end awkwardness. They're the cheapest thing a person can give you.
- **Example:** "That's a great idea!" → "Thanks. How do you handle it now?"
- **Founder application:** Log compliments as drift, not as wins.
- **Common mistake:** Ending a meeting warm and calling it validated.
- **Rule:** *A compliment is an alarm, not a reward.*

### 4. Fluff is honest but useless
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** "Always," "never," "would," "might" aren't lies. They're what you get when you ask a question with no factual answer.
- **Explanation:** The customer is trying to help. Your question gave them nothing concrete to help with.
- **Example:** "I always forget to follow up." → "When did that last bite you?"
- **Founder application:** Anchor every generic to a specific instance. If none exists, the generic was empty.
- **Common mistake:** Writing down the generic as a finding.
- **Rule:** *Every "always" needs a "last time."*

### 5. Ideas are clues, not instructions
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** A feature request is a symptom of a motivation. Build for the motivation.
- **Explanation:** Customers know their problem; they don't know solutions. Their proposed feature is their guess. Your job is the "why" beneath it.
- **Example:** "Add a deposit system." → "Why?" → "Reminders don't work." → "Have you tried deposits?" → "The owner's scared to."
- **Founder application:** Never write a feature request in the roadmap without the motive next to it.
- **Common mistake:** Building the feature list.
- **Rule:** *Dig for the why; ignore the what.*

### 6. Cost is the only currency of truth
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** Interest is measured in what someone gives up: time, reputation, money.
- **Explanation:** Words are free, so words carry no information about willingness. Only sacrifice does.
- **Example:** "Keep me posted" (free) vs. "Here's my CFO's email, tell her I sent you" (reputation).
- **Founder application:** End every solution-stage conversation with an ask that costs them something.
- **Common mistake:** Confusing enthusiasm with commitment.
- **Rule:** *If it didn't cost them anything, it isn't a signal.*

### 7. Love bad news
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** A negative answer closes a wrong path cheaply. A "meh" is a verdict. Both are wins.
- **Explanation:** The expensive outcome isn't a dead idea; it's six months building a dead idea because nobody said "meh" loud enough.
- **Example:** "Would you let AI send replies unsupervised?" "Absolutely not." Product version killed in one sentence, before it was built.
- **Founder application:** After each conversation, ask "what weakened my idea?" If nothing, you weren't asking.
- **Common mistake:** Arguing with the bad news.
- **Rule:** *Bad news early is the cheapest thing you'll ever buy.*

### 8. Ask the scary question first
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** In every conversation there's one question you don't want to ask because you suspect the answer. Ask it, early.
- **Explanation:** Your dread is a signal that you already half-know. Avoiding the question doesn't change the answer, it just delays learning it.
- **Example:** "Is there actually budget for this, and who controls it?"
- **Founder application:** Write the scary question at the top of your prep. Ask it in the first five minutes.
- **Common mistake:** Saving it for the end and running out of time.
- **Rule:** *If you're scared to ask it, that's the one to ask.*

### 9. Look before you zoom
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** Confirm the problem is on their list before diving into it.
- **Explanation:** If you introduce the problem, they'll politely discuss it. If they introduce it, it's real.
- **Example:** "What's the hardest part of running support?" → "Hiring." (Not tickets. Hiring.)
- **Founder application:** Open broad. Ask about your problem last, and note whether it came up on its own.
- **Common mistake:** Opening with "tell me about your problems with X."
- **Rule:** *Let them rank before you dig.*

### 10. Some problems don't matter
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** People will complain at length about problems they'd never spend a dollar to fix.
- **Explanation:** Annoyance and priority are different. The test is action: have they done anything?
- **Example:** Twenty minutes on how bad reporting is; zero tools tried, zero hours invested.
- **Founder application:** For every named problem, ask "what have you tried?" No action means no priority.
- **Common mistake:** Sizing the market on how much people complain.
- **Rule:** *Complaint without action is entertainment.*

### 11. Their current solution is your real competitor
**[Synthesis, from the book's "how are you dealing with it now?"]**
- **Principle:** You compete with the spreadsheet, the intern, and doing nothing, not with the other startup.
- **Explanation:** The current solution reveals what they'll tolerate, what they'll pay, and what switching costs them.
- **Example:** "The bigger chains just hire a manager for this." That's the value ceiling: one salary.
- **Founder application:** Always ask what they use now, what it costs all-in, and why they haven't switched.
- **Common mistake:** Not asking, and pricing against a competitor's pricing page.
- **Rule:** *Price against the workaround, not the wishlist.*

### 12. Casual beats formal
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** Formality creates performance. Performance creates politeness. Politeness kills data.
- **Explanation:** A scheduled interview makes both parties feel obligations. A chat at an event doesn't.
- **Example:** Three minutes with a café owner at a roaster's open house yields more than a 30-minute Zoom.
- **Founder application:** Be where they are, with three questions memorized.
- **Common mistake:** Thinking you need warm intros and calendar invites to learn anything.
- **Rule:** *If it feels like a favor, it's too formal.*

### 13. Meetings succeed or fail; there's no "went well"
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** A solution-stage meeting either ends in commitment/advancement or it failed.
- **Explanation:** "Went well" is the founder's feeling. Commitment is the customer's action. Only the second exists outside your head.
- **Example:** "Sounds great, keep me posted" = fail. "Come Tuesday at 3" = success.
- **Founder application:** Decide the ask before the meeting. Make it. Log the answer as pass/fail.
- **Common mistake:** A pipeline full of zombies who always say yes and never do anything.
- **Rule:** *No commitment, no success. Log it as a fail.*

### 14. Objections mean they're trying it on
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** A specific objection is someone mentally using your product and hitting a wall.
- **Explanation:** People don't object to things they've dismissed. They smile and wait for you to leave.
- **Example:** "That won't work unless subs submit through it." Now you know the adoption problem.
- **Founder application:** Ask for objections explicitly: "What would make this not work for you?"
- **Common mistake:** Defending against the objection instead of mining it.
- **Rule:** *A meeting with no objections is a meeting with no interest.*

### 15. Inconsistent signals mean too broad a segment
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** When every conversation contradicts the last, you're mixing populations.
- **Explanation:** A segment is a group with a consistent top problem. If problems scatter, the group isn't a segment.
- **Example:** "Course creators" = hobbyists (problem: sales) + full-timers (problem: support) + teams (problem: logistics).
- **Founder application:** After five conversations, tabulate attributes against top problem. Slice on whatever predicts it.
- **Common mistake:** Adding features for everyone.
- **Rule:** *When answers scatter, narrow the who, don't widen the what.*

### 16. The founder is the bottleneck
**[Book]**
- **Principle:** Customer learning that lives in one head doesn't exist for the team.
- **Explanation:** Secondhand summaries lose the quotes, the emotion, and the surprises. Team decisions then default to whoever argues best.
- **Example:** "Trust me, customers want X" vs. a shared notes doc with eight quotes and a killed assumption.
- **Founder application:** Everyone builds, everyone talks to customers. Raw notes shared. Weekly review.
- **Common mistake:** The CEO does all the calls and the engineers build from memos.
- **Rule:** *If only you heard it, the team didn't learn it.*

### 17. Facts and interpretations live in different columns
**[Synthesis, from the book's note-taking discipline]**
- **Principle:** What they said is data. What you think it means is a hypothesis.
- **Explanation:** Mixing them makes every disagreement a fight about reality instead of a discussion about meaning.
- **Example:** Fact: "She said the last purchase took four months and Miguel signed." Interpretation: "Finance is the buyer."
- **Founder application:** Two columns in every notes doc. Teammates may edit only the right one.
- **Common mistake:** Notes that say "they're interested."
- **Rule:** *Quote in one column, guess in the other.*

### 18. Your idea is the contaminant
**[Synthesis, the book's central move stated as a mechanism]**
- **Principle:** The moment your idea enters the conversation, the customer stops being a witness and becomes a judge.
- **Explanation:** Witnesses report what happened. Judges of a friend's work are lenient. You want the witness.
- **Example:** Every good example in this document keeps the idea out until the discovery boxes are full.
- **Founder application:** Keep your idea in one sentence you've decided not to say until you've earned it.
- **Common mistake:** "Just quickly explaining what we're building so they have context."
- **Rule:** *Keep the idea out of the room until you know whether it matters.*

# Part 17 — THE MOM TEST → FOUNDER CONVERSATION SKILL

**[Synthesis]** Everything above, unified into one skill, organized by the ten questions a founder needs answered.

## How should a founder think before talking to someone?

You are a detective investigating someone's life, not a salesperson auditioning an idea. Your idea is a hypothesis you're trying to disprove cheaply. The person across from you is the world's foremost expert on their own past and a poor guesser about everything else. Bad news is a gift; surprises are the goal; comfort is the warning. You're a little scared of one question, and you're going to ask it first.

## What should they ask?

Questions about **them**, about **the past**, answerable with a **fact or a story**:
- **Broad opener** to let them rank: "What's the hardest part of…?"
- **Anchor:** "When did that last happen?"
- **Expand:** "Walk me through it."
- **Cost:** "What did that cost you, in hours or dollars? What happened as a result?"
- **Frequency:** "How many times last month?"
- **Current solution:** "How do you handle it today? What have you tried? Why didn't it stick?"
- **Spending:** "Have you ever paid for anything to fix this?"
- **Decision:** "Who decided on the last purchase like this?"
- **Urgency:** "What's making this matter now?"
- **Priority:** "Where does it rank?"
- **Close:** "Who else should I talk to? What should I have asked?"

## What should they avoid asking?

Anything with **would, could, might, think, like, interested, or a rating.** Anything whose subject is your product. Anything answerable with a polite yes. Anything that names the problem for them. "Would you use / pay / buy / recommend" and "do you like / think it's a good idea" are all the same question, and it's the one your mom will lie about.

## What should they listen for?

Past-tense specifics. Money. Time. Workarounds. Emotion. Urgency triggers. Constraints ("can't," "IT won't"). The decision path (names, sign-offs). Objections. Contradictions between claims and behavior. Absences: no incident, no number, no spend, no action.

Alarms: compliments, fluff words, your own voice for more than 30% of the time, no surprises yet.

## How should they interpret answers?

Place each on the Signal Strength Ladder. The higher, the more it cost them to produce:
- **Rungs 1–7** (compliments, opinions, intentions, prompted agreement, ideas, generics): **no evidence.**
- **Rungs 8–15** (specific incidents, emotion, consequences, objections, search, workarounds, time and money spent): **the problem is real.**
- **Rungs 16–19** (time, intros, resources, money committed to you): **the solution is wanted.**

Facts in one column. Interpretations in the other. A problem is real when five people in one segment name it unprompted, describe recent instances, have spent time or money on it, and rank it high. A solution is wanted when people give something up for it. Nothing else counts.

## How should they follow up?

Classify what was said (generic, future, opinion, compliment, idea, incident, emotion, constraint, objection, name). Identify what you're missing (instance, story, frequency, cost, consequence, action, solution, owner, urgency, priority, motive, hardness). Ask for the most important missing thing. Under pressure: "When did that last happen?" is never wrong.

After the conversation: every name within 24 hours; a thank-you that quotes them; commitments confirmed in writing same day.

## How do they recognize real evidence?

Three questions of any signal:
1. Past or future? Future is worth almost nothing.
2. Did it cost them anything? Free signals are weak.
3. Is it specific enough to be wrong? Unfalsifiable statements carry no information.

Real evidence is a dated incident with a number attached, a workaround they built, money they've spent, or a commitment they've made. Everything else is atmosphere.

## How do they avoid fooling themselves?

- Write assumptions as falsifiable sentences before talking to anyone. Write what would kill each one.
- Ask the scary question early.
- Deflect compliments, anchor fluff, dig beneath ideas, every time.
- Keep the idea out until discovery is complete.
- Score every conversation with the scorecard. Track the lowest category.
- Run a debrief with a teammate who reads your notes and finds the fluff you counted as fact.
- Ask weekly: did anything change a decision? Did anything scare us? Did anyone but the founder hear a customer?
- Log solution-stage meetings as pass/fail on commitment. "Went well" is not a category.

## How do they know when they have enough information?

Within a segment, when: the last three people described the same top problem unprompted in similar words; you can predict what the next person will say; you know the current solution, its cost, and its failure mode; you've heard the same objection twice; and nothing has surprised you in several conversations. That's the signal to stop discovering and start showing something with an ask attached. If commitments come, keep going. If they don't, the "why not" is your next discovery target.

## How do conversations translate into product and business decisions?

| Decision | Made from |
|---|---|
| **Who to build for** | The segment where five people named the same problem, unprompted, with recent instances |
| **What to build first** | The step in their workflow where the most time or money is lost, per their own numbers |
| **What not to build** | Feature requests whose "why" was weak, and anything a hard constraint kills |
| **Pricing** | The all-in cost of their current solution, tested with an actual ask for money |
| **Positioning** | Their exact words for the problem and the consequence |
| **Who to sell to** | Whoever decided on the last purchase, not whoever complained |
| **Urgency in messaging** | The trigger events they named (audit, growth, new boss, disaster) |
| **When to pivot** | When three conversations killed the central assumption and no re-slice rescues it |
| **When to stop discovering** | When surprises stop and commitments start |

Every decision should trace to named conversations. Any decision that can't is opinion wearing evidence's clothes.

## The skill in one paragraph

Before you talk to anyone, write down what you believe and what would prove you wrong. Walk in curious about their life, with your idea in your pocket and one scary question at the top of your list. Open broad, let them rank their problems, then anchor whatever they name to the last time it happened and follow it down to time, money, and consequences. Learn what they do about it now, what they've tried, who decides, and why it matters today. Deflect every compliment, anchor every generic, dig beneath every idea, and say nothing for most of the conversation. Only when you can fill every discovery box do you show them anything, and then you ask for something that costs them: time, an intro, or money. Write down what they said and what you think separately. Score yourself. Share it with your team. Update your assumptions. Follow up on every name. When five people in the same segment tell you the same thing without prompting and start giving up things to get your solution, you've learned enough. Until then, you're still learning, and that's the whole job.
