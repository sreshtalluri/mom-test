# Part 3 — The Questioning Framework

## 3.1 The core rule behind every good question

**[Book]** A good question is one your mom couldn't lie to you about. **[Synthesis]** Operationally, that means every good question has three properties:

1. **Subject: them.** It's about their life, work, or past, not your product.
2. **Tense: past or present.** It asks what happened or what is, never what would be.
3. **Answer type: a fact or a story.** It can be answered with a specific event, a number, a name, or a description of current behavior. Never with a rating, an opinion, or a promise.

Every category below applies these three tests.

## 3.2 Question categories

### Category 1 — Understanding the problem

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | Whether the problem exists in their world, how they describe it in their own words, and whether it's on their radar without prompting. |
| **Good questions** | "What's the hardest part of [their job/activity]?" · "What's the most annoying thing that happened this week in [domain]?" · "Walk me through the last time [problem] came up." · "What do you wish was easier about [domain]?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Is [problem] a big issue for you?" (leading) · "Do you struggle with X?" (yes/no, invites polite agreement) · "Would it be helpful if X were easier?" (future, hypothetical) |
| **Strong signals** | They name your problem unprompted. They describe a specific, recent incident with details. They show emotion. They use vivid language ("nightmare," "I dread"). |
| **Weak signals** | They agree it's a problem only after you name it. They describe it generically. They shrug. They say "I guess" or "sometimes." |
| **Follow-ups** | "When did that last happen?" · "What happened as a result?" · "Where does that rank against the other things on your plate?" |

### Category 2 — Understanding existing behavior

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | What they actually do today, step by step, including the parts they don't think of as "the process." |
| **Good questions** | "How do you handle that right now?" · "Show me how you did it last time." · "What does a typical [day/week/instance] look like, start to finish?" · "Who's involved?" · "What tools are open on your screen when you do this?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Would you change how you do it?" · "Do you think your process is efficient?" (opinion) · "Do you use any tools for this?" (yes/no, shallow) |
| **Strong signals** | A detailed, specific sequence with named tools and people. Complaints about specific steps. Steps they're embarrassed by ("this is dumb, but…"). |
| **Weak signals** | "It depends." A high-level summary with no steps. "It's fine." |
| **Follow-ups** | "What's the step you hate most?" · "What happens if you skip that step?" · "Has it always been done this way?" |

### Category 3 — Understanding frequency

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | How often the problem occurs, so you can size it and judge whether a solution would be habit-forming. |
| **Good questions** | "How many times did that happen last month?" · "When was the time before last?" · "Is it daily, weekly, seasonal, or random?" · "Can you look at your calendar/inbox and count?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Does this happen a lot?" (invites "yeah, all the time" fluff) · "Would you use it every day?" (future) |
| **Strong signals** | A number, or a countable pattern ("every Friday close," "every new hire"). They can point to the last three occurrences. |
| **Weak signals** | "All the time" with no instances. "Pretty often." A number that shrinks when you ask for the last three. |
| **Follow-ups** | "Tell me about the most recent one." · "Does it cluster around anything, like month-end or launches?" · "Is it getting more or less frequent?" |

### Category 4 — Understanding severity

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | How bad it is when it happens. Consequences, not feelings. |
| **Good questions** | "What happened as a result?" · "What did it cost you, in hours or dollars?" · "Who found out?" · "What's the worst it's ever gotten?" · "What did you have to skip or delay because of it?" |
| **Bad questions** | "On a scale of 1–10, how painful is this?" (ratings are opinions) · "Is it a big deal?" · "Would it be a huge relief if this went away?" |
| **Strong signals** | Concrete consequences: lost revenue, lost customer, missed deadline, a reprimand, a weekend lost. Emotion when recounting it. |
| **Weak signals** | "It's annoying." "It's not ideal." No downstream consequence named. |
| **Follow-ups** | "And then what happened?" · "Did anyone above you notice?" · "Is that typical, or was that the bad case?" |

### Category 5 — Understanding current solutions

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | What they're using now, including manual workarounds, and how they feel about it. Their current solution is your real competitor and sets your value ceiling. |
| **Good questions** | "What are you using for this today?" · "What else have you tried?" · "How did you pick that?" · "What does it do well? What do you hate?" · "Why haven't you switched?" · "What did you use before that?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Have you looked at [competitor]?" (leading) · "Would you switch if something better existed?" (future, and everyone says yes) |
| **Strong signals** | Named tools, named vendors, spreadsheets they built, people they hired. A history of trying and abandoning things. Specific complaints. |
| **Weak signals** | "Nothing really." (Either the problem doesn't matter or they haven't looked, both bad.) "We manage." |
| **Follow-ups** | "What made you stop using that?" · "What would have to be true for you to switch?" · "How much does the current thing cost, all-in?" |

### Category 6 — Understanding spending

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | Whether money has ever moved toward this problem, and how much. Past spending is the strongest predictor of future spending. |
| **Good questions** | "What are you paying for the current solution?" · "Have you ever bought anything for this?" · "What's the total cost of the workaround, including people's time?" · "What did you spend on the last thing you tried?" |
| **Bad questions** | "How much would you pay?" · "Would you pay $X?" · "Is budget a concern?" |
| **Strong signals** | Dollar figures. Subscriptions. Contractors hired. Headcount partially dedicated. "We spent $X and it didn't work." |
| **Weak signals** | "We'd definitely pay for a good solution." No historical spending at all. |
| **Follow-ups** | "Where did that money come from, which budget line?" · "Was that a hard sell internally?" · "Would you spend that again?" |

### Category 7 — Understanding decision-making

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | Who buys, who approves, who blocks, who uses. In B2B, the person you're talking to is often not the buyer. |
| **Good questions** | "Who decided on the last tool you bought for this?" · "Walk me through how that purchase happened." · "Who would need to sign off?" · "Who could veto it?" · "How long did the last purchase take, from first conversation to signed?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Could you buy this?" · "Are you the decision-maker?" (people overstate) · "Would your company be interested?" |
| **Strong signals** | Named people and roles. A described process with steps and timing. Awareness of budget cycles. |
| **Weak signals** | "I'd just buy it." (Often untrue.) "It'd be easy to get approved." Vagueness about who else is involved. |
| **Follow-ups** | "What made the last purchase get stuck?" · "Can you introduce me to [named person]?" · "What does [approver] care most about?" |

### Category 8 — Understanding workflow

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | Where the problem sits in the larger flow of their work, what comes before and after, and what constraints the surrounding system imposes. |
| **Good questions** | "What triggers this task?" · "What happens right before? Right after?" · "What has to be true before you can start?" · "What other systems does this touch?" · "Who's waiting on you when this is done?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Would this fit into your workflow?" · "Could you see using this daily?" |
| **Strong signals** | A clear chain of dependencies. Named upstream and downstream systems and people. Bottlenecks identified. |
| **Weak signals** | Task described in isolation. "It's pretty standalone." |
| **Follow-ups** | "Which of those steps is the slowest?" · "Where does it usually break?" · "If this step disappeared, what would change downstream?" |

### Category 9 — Understanding urgency

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | Whether this needs solving now, or is a someday thing. Urgency is what converts interest into purchase. |
| **Good questions** | "What's driving you to look at this now, if anything?" · "What happens if this isn't solved by end of quarter?" · "Has anything changed recently that made this worse?" · "Is there a deadline this connects to?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Is this urgent?" · "Would you want this soon?" |
| **Strong signals** | An external forcing event: audit, growth, new regulation, new boss, a recent disaster. A date. Active searching. |
| **Weak signals** | "It's been like this for years." (Chronic but tolerated.) No trigger. "Eventually." |
| **Follow-ups** | "What made it become a priority?" · "Who's pushing for it?" · "What have you done in the last 30 days about it?" |

### Category 10 — Understanding consequences

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | The downstream effects of the problem, which are where the real value of solving it lives. |
| **Good questions** | "What did that cause?" · "Who was affected?" · "What did you have to do to recover?" · "Has it ever cost you a customer, a deal, or a person?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Would solving this save you money?" · "Would it be valuable?" |
| **Strong signals** | Second-order effects: churn, overtime, hiring, reputational hits, regulatory exposure. |
| **Weak signals** | Effects stop at "it was annoying." |
| **Follow-ups** | "And then what?" (repeat until you hit money, time, or people) · "Does leadership know?" |

### Category 11 — Understanding priorities

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | Where this problem ranks against everything else competing for their time and money. |
| **Good questions** | "What are the top three things you're trying to fix this quarter?" · "If you could only fix one thing, what would it be?" · "What did you actually spend last month's discretionary budget on?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Is this a priority?" · "Would you prioritize this?" |
| **Strong signals** | Your problem appears in their top three unprompted. They've allocated time or budget to it. |
| **Weak signals** | Your problem isn't on the list until you mention it. "It's on the list" with no rank. |
| **Follow-ups** | "What's ahead of it, and why?" · "What would have to happen for it to move up?" |

### Category 12 — Understanding alternatives

| | |
|---|---|
| **Trying to learn** | The full set of ways they could address the problem, including doing nothing, hiring, or using an adjacent tool. Your real competition is often "the intern" or "the spreadsheet." |
| **Good questions** | "If you had to solve this next week without a new tool, what would you do?" · "Has anyone at your company proposed a fix?" · "Have you considered just hiring for it?" · "What do your peers at other companies do?" |
| **Bad questions** | "Would you prefer our solution over [competitor]?" · "Is there anything else out there?" (they won't know) |
| **Strong signals** | They've evaluated multiple options and can compare them. They know what "doing nothing" costs. |
| **Weak signals** | They haven't considered alternatives at all. |
| **Follow-ups** | "Why didn't you go with that?" · "What would make hiring the wrong answer?" |

## 3.3 The universal follow-up set

**[Book + Synthesis]** Regardless of category, these seven follow-ups rescue almost any answer:

1. **"When did that last happen?"** — anchors generics to an instance.
2. **"Walk me through it."** — converts a summary into a story.
3. **"What did you do about it?"** — tests whether it mattered.
4. **"What did that cost you?"** — surfaces time and money.
5. **"Why?"** — surfaces the motivation behind a wish or a feature request.
6. **"And then what?"** — surfaces consequences.
7. **"Who else?"** — surfaces decision-makers and next conversations.

# Part 4 — Questions That Sound Good but Are Actually Bad

**[Synthesis, built on the book's good/bad question list]** Each entry: why it's bad, what it accidentally encourages, and the reframe.

| # | The question | Why it's bad | What it encourages | Reframe |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Would you use this?" | Future tense, about your product, costs nothing to say yes. | Polite yes; aspirational self-image. | "What do you use for this now? When did you last use it?" |
| 2 | "Would you pay for this?" | Hypothetical spending. Everyone's generous with imaginary money. | "Sure, probably." | "What have you paid for to solve this before? How much?" |
| 3 | "Do you like this idea?" | Asks for an opinion on something they can't evaluate. Directly invites the mom-lie. | Compliments. | Delete it. Ask about their last experience with the problem instead. |
| 4 | "Would this solve your problem?" | Assumes the problem, assumes they can predict a solution's effect. | Agreement with your framing. | "How are you solving it today? What's wrong with that?" |
| 5 | "How much would you pay?" | People don't know, and they'll lowball or flatter. | A random number. | "What does the current approach cost you, all-in?" then later, an actual price with an actual ask. |
| 6 | "Would your company buy this?" | They usually don't know their company's purchasing reality, and it's future. | "I think so." | "Walk me through the last tool your team bought. Who decided, how long did it take?" |
| 7 | "Do you think this is a good idea?" | Pure opinion. | Encouragement. | "What's the biggest problem in [domain] right now?" and see if your idea's problem is even mentioned. |
| 8 | "Would you recommend something like this?" | Hypothetical social behavior. | "Of course!" | "Who did you last recommend a tool to, and why?" |
| 9 | "Would you use an app that did X?" | Same as #1 with a feature bolted on. Leading. | Yes. | "How do you do X today? Tell me about the last time." |
| 10 | "Do you have a problem with X?" | Yes/no, and you've named the problem for them. | Polite agreement. | "What's the most annoying part of [broader area]?" |
| 11 | "How often do you deal with X?" | Sounds behavioral, but invites "all the time" fluff. | Vague frequency inflation. | "How many times did it happen last month? Tell me about the most recent." |
| 12 | "What features would you want?" | Turns them into a co-designer. Produces a wish list with no priority. | A feature list you'll build blindly. | "What's the worst part of the current way? What have you tried to fix it?" |
| 13 | "Is this something you'd be interested in?" | "Interested" is free. | "Sure, keep me posted." | "Would you spend 30 minutes next week trying a rough version on a real task?" |
| 14 | "What would your ideal solution look like?" | Invites fantasy. Only useful if you dig for the why. | An imaginary product. | Ask it only as a bridge: "…and why would that matter to you?" |
| 15 | "Would this save you time?" | Leading and future. | "Definitely." | "How long did the last one take, start to finish?" |
| 16 | "Is [competitor] not working for you?" | Leading, and invites complaining that isn't backed by switching behavior. | Gripes without action. | "What are you using now? Why haven't you switched?" |
| 17 | "On a scale of 1–10, how painful is this?" | Ratings are opinions with numbers on them. | A meaningless 7. | "What did it cost you the last time it happened?" |
| 18 | "Would you switch if we were cheaper/better?" | Everyone says yes to hypothetical improvement. | Yes. | "When did you last switch a tool in this area? What made you do it?" |
| 19 | "Do you think other people have this problem?" | Asks them to guess about others. | Speculation. | "Who do you know who deals with this? Could you introduce me?" |
| 20 | "Would you be an early adopter?" | Flattering identity question. | "Absolutely!" | "Can I put a 30-minute setup call on your calendar for Tuesday?" |
| 21 | "Does this make sense?" | About your explanation, invites politeness. | Nodding. | Don't explain. Ask. |
| 22 | "Would you be willing to give feedback later?" | Costs nothing to agree to. | Zombie leads. | "Can we book the feedback call now? What date?" |
| 23 | "If this existed, would it change how you work?" | Deep hypothetical. | Imaginative yes. | "What changed the last time you adopted a new tool? What broke?" |
| 24 | "Is price the main issue?" | Leading, and price is the easy answer. | "Yeah, price." | "What stopped you from buying the last thing you evaluated?" |
| 25 | "What do you think of the design?" | Opinion on aesthetics before behavior is understood. | Compliments. | Watch them try to complete a task. Ask what they expected to happen. |

**The pattern across all 25:** each one asks the customer to predict, judge, or imagine. The reframe always asks them to **remember** or to **act**.

## 4.1 A test you can run on any question in real time

**[Synthesis]** Before you ask, check:

- Does it contain "would," "could," "might," "think," "like," or "interested"? → Rewrite.
- Is the grammatical subject your product? → Rewrite.
- Could the person answer it honestly with "yes" and it still mean nothing? → Rewrite.
- Could your mom answer it in a way that protects your feelings? → Rewrite.

If it survives, ask it and shut up.
