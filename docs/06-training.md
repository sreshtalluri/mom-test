# Part 12 — Training System

**[Synthesis]** Five levels. Each has skills, exercises, practice conversations, mistakes, evaluation, and graduation criteria. Don't skip levels; each assumes the reflexes of the one below.

---

## Level 1 — Beginner: Basic Question Discipline

**Skills**
- Recognize and eliminate future-tense, opinion, and product-centered questions.
- Ask about the past.
- Stay silent after asking.

**Exercises**
1. **Question audit.** Write your 10 current interview questions. Rewrite each to be about them, about the past, and answerable with a fact. Delete any that can't be.
2. **The would-detector.** For one week, every time you hear yourself say "would," "could," or "do you think," stop and rephrase out loud.
3. **Silence drill.** Ask a friend a question and count to five before speaking again. Do it ten times.
4. **Mom drill.** Explain your idea to a family member. Then don't. Instead, ask them about the last time they hit the problem. Compare what you learned.

**Practice conversation**
Pick any friend with a job. Ask only: "What's the hardest part of your job? When did that last happen? What did you do?" Nothing else. Write down three facts you learned.

**Mistakes to avoid**
- Filling silence with your idea.
- Asking two questions at once.
- Accepting "yeah, it's a pain" as an answer.

**Evaluation criteria**
- Zero future-tense questions in a 10-minute conversation.
- Talked less than 30% of the time.
- Three concrete past facts captured.

**Graduation**
Three consecutive conversations with zero "would/could/think" questions and at least three specific incidents recorded.

---

## Level 2 — Intermediate: Behavioral Questioning and Listening

**Skills**
- Anchor generics to instances reflexively.
- Dig beneath feature requests to motives.
- Deflect compliments without losing rapport.
- Follow tangents and emotional shifts.
- Extract time, money, and frequency figures.

**Exercises**
1. **Reflex drill.** Have a partner throw fluff at you ("I always struggle with that," "I'd definitely buy it," "you should add dark mode"). Respond within one second with the correct reflex. Twenty reps.
2. **Five-deep.** Take any complaint and ask "why" or "and then what" five times. Note where you hit money, time, or people.
3. **Quote capture.** After each conversation, write five verbatim phrases. Compare to your paraphrase and note what the paraphrase lost.
4. **Emotion tracking.** In one conversation, mark every tone shift. Did you dig into each one?

**Practice conversation**
Target someone in your actual segment. Goal: leave with one time figure, one money figure, one frequency figure, and one workaround. If any is missing, the conversation isn't done.

**Mistakes to avoid**
- Anchoring so aggressively it feels like cross-examination. Soften: "Just so I get it right, when was the last time?"
- Digging beneath a feature request and then building the feature anyway.
- Nodding enthusiastically (it's a form of leading).

**Evaluation criteria**
- Every generic in the transcript was anchored.
- Every idea was followed by "why."
- All four figures (time, money, frequency, workaround) captured.

**Graduation**
Five conversations in a row hitting all four figures, and a partner reviewing your notes can't find an unanchored generic.

---

## Level 3 — Advanced: Detecting Strong vs. Weak Signals

**Skills**
- Place every statement on the Signal Strength Ladder in real time.
- Recognize false positives while they're happening.
- Ask the scary question early.
- Distinguish discovery evidence from validation evidence.
- Spot contradictions and surface them gently.

**Exercises**
1. **Ladder scoring.** Transcribe (or record with permission) a conversation. Score every customer statement 1–19. Count how many are 8+. That's your evidence count.
2. **False-positive hunt.** Review your last ten conversations' notes. Highlight every line you treated as positive. Reclassify. How many survive?
3. **Scary question log.** Before each conversation, write the scary question and your feared answer. After, write the real answer. Track how often fear was justified.
4. **Contradiction spotting.** In three conversations, find at least one contradiction between stated priority and actual behavior. Practice surfacing it: "Earlier you said X, but Y. Help me understand."

**Practice conversation**
Solution-stage conversation with a real prospect. Deliberately withhold the pitch until every discovery box is filled. Then flip and ask for a commitment. Score the ending: commitment, advancement, or fail.

**Mistakes to avoid**
- Inflating a rung-8 (specific incident) into validation. It's discovery evidence only.
- Treating a specific objection as rejection. It's engagement.
- Skipping the scary question because the conversation was going well.

**Evaluation criteria**
- Post-conversation ladder placement matches an independent reviewer's placement.
- Scary question asked in the first third of the conversation.
- Commitment asked for, out loud, in every solution-stage conversation.

**Graduation**
Ten conversations with an evidence count (rung 8+) of five or more each, and at least three commitments (rung 16+) obtained across them.

---

## Level 4 — Expert: Natural Conversations Without a Script

**Skills**
- Run the whole decision tree from memory, adapting order to what they say.
- Keep it casual: get the key answer in five minutes at an event.
- Use the outreach framing naturally in writing and speech.
- Move between discovery and solution mode in one conversation without contaminating discovery.
- Recover from your own drift instantly.

**Exercises**
1. **Five-minute drill.** At a meetup, get a specific incident, a cost, and a current solution from a stranger in under five minutes. Repeat with three people.
2. **Script burn.** Do a conversation with no notes visible. Reconstruct the decision tree path afterward.
3. **Flip timing.** Record where in the conversation you flipped to solution. Was every discovery box filled? If not, you flipped early.
4. **Outreach A/B.** Send ten messages with the five-part framing and ten without. Compare reply rates.

**Practice conversation**
Attend an event in your segment's world. Have five conversations. Book two follow-ups. Get three names. Do it without a single "would."

**Mistakes to avoid**
- Getting so smooth you stop being genuinely curious. People can tell.
- Running the tree mechanically instead of following the person's energy.
- Confusing "natural" with "unprepared." The three questions are still written down beforehand.

**Evaluation criteria**
- Someone observing can't tell it's an interview.
- Decision tree fully traversed without visible reference.
- At least two follow-ups booked per event.

**Graduation**
Twenty conversations across at least three venues (cold outreach, event, warm intro) with consistent evidence counts, plus a documented flip that produced a commitment.

---

## Level 5 — Founder-Level Mastery: Conversations Drive Strategy

**Skills**
- Slice segments based on conversation patterns.
- Turn conversation evidence into product, pricing, positioning, and sales decisions.
- Run the team process: prep, notes, debrief, assumption tracker, weekly review.
- Detect when the team is going through the motions.
- Know when to stop discovering and start selling, and vice versa.

**Exercises**
1. **Segment table.** After every five conversations, tabulate attributes against top problem. Identify the slicing variable. Narrow. Repeat until three consecutive people give the same answer.
2. **Decision trace.** For each product decision this month, write which conversations justify it. Any decision with no conversation behind it gets flagged.
3. **Pricing from cost.** Compute the total cost of the current solution across five customers. Price against it. Test with an actual ask for money.
4. **Team rotation.** Every builder does one conversation a week. You review their notes using the scorecard.
5. **Motions audit.** Monthly, answer the Chapter 8 "going through the motions" checks honestly.

**Practice conversation**
Not one conversation: a batch. Design a five-conversation batch to kill or confirm your single most uncertain assumption. Run it. Write the decision it produced.

**Mistakes to avoid**
- Becoming the bottleneck: holding all customer knowledge in your head.
- Letting conversation evidence lose to the loudest opinion in the room.
- Continuing discovery past the point of surprise because it's more comfortable than asking for money.
- Ignoring the "meh" because you've already invested.

**Evaluation criteria**
- Every roadmap item traces to named conversations.
- Segment definition is one sentence and has been narrowed at least twice.
- Team members can quote customers.
- Assumption tracker has entries marked "killed."

**Graduation**
A product or pricing decision that you changed because of conversation evidence, against your prior preference, and that later proved right by commitment (time, intro, or money).

# Part 13 — Practice Scenarios

**[Synthesis]** Twelve scenarios. Each: hypothesis, customer background, opening, bad founder response, good founder response, why, key signal, next question.

---

### Scenario 1 — B2B SaaS: Sales pipeline hygiene

**Hypothesis:** Sales managers at 20–50 rep companies waste hours cleaning CRM data and would pay for automated hygiene.
**Customer:** VP Sales at a 35-rep fintech. Uses Salesforce. Ten years in sales.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** Yeah, our Salesforce is a mess. Everyone's is.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** Right, that's exactly what we're solving. We automatically detect stale deals and duplicate contacts. Would that help?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** What did "mess" cost you most recently?

**Why better:** The bad response accepts a generic ("everyone's is") as confirmation and pitches. The good one anchors to a consequence, which will reveal whether "mess" is an aesthetic complaint or a revenue problem.
**Key signal to find:** A forecast miss, a lost deal, or a specific board meeting where bad data hurt. If none, the mess is tolerated.
**Next best question:** "Who's job is it to clean it now, and how long do they spend?"

---

### Scenario 2 — Consumer app: Meal planning

**Hypothesis:** Busy parents want a weekly meal-planning app that generates a shopping list.
**Customer:** Parent of two, works full-time, cooks most weeknights.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** Honestly I'd love something that just told me what to cook.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** That's literally what we're building! Would you pay $5 a month for it?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** What did you do about dinner last night, start to finish?

**Why better:** "I'd love something" is intention, weight zero. The good response gets an instance and will reveal the real behavior (leftovers? takeout? a rotation of six meals?) which usually contradicts the stated wish.
**Key signal to find:** Whether they've ever tried a meal-planning app, subscription, or system, and why they stopped. Most have, and quit within two weeks. That's the real problem.
**Next best question:** "Have you tried any apps or services for this before? What happened?"

---

### Scenario 3 — Education: Tutoring marketplace

**Hypothesis:** Parents of high schoolers struggle to find vetted math tutors and would pay a platform fee.
**Customer:** Parent of a 10th grader who's currently getting a B- in algebra.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** We looked into tutoring but it's so expensive.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** Our platform would make it more affordable by connecting you directly. Would you use that?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** What did you look into, and what did it cost?

**Why better:** The bad response argues with the objection and pitches. The good one treats "expensive" as a data point to be quantified, and "looked into" as behavior to be expanded.
**Key signal to find:** Whether they've actually paid for any tutoring, and the price that made them stop. If they've never paid at any price, "expensive" may mean "not a priority."
**Next best question:** "What's the grade at which you'd definitely do something?"

---

### Scenario 4 — Healthcare: Prior authorization

**Hypothesis:** Small specialty clinics lose staff hours to insurance prior-auth paperwork and would pay for automation.
**Customer:** Office manager at a 3-physician dermatology practice.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** Prior auths are the bane of my existence.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** We've heard that a lot. We're building an AI that fills them out automatically. What features would you want?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** How many did you do last week, and how long did each one take?

**Why better:** Strong emotional language is a doorway, not a conclusion. The good response converts it into frequency and time, which tell you the size of the prize. The bad one asks for a feature list before knowing the workflow.
**Key signal to find:** Hours per week, plus whether they've ever used a service, hired a person, or changed which insurers they accept because of it. Any of those means it's worth real money.
**Next best question:** "Have you ever paid anyone or anything to take this off your plate?"

---

### Scenario 5 — Fitness: Personal trainer scheduling

**Hypothesis:** Independent personal trainers lose income to no-shows and would pay for a booking tool with deposits.
**Customer:** Trainer with 25 weekly clients, works out of two gyms.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** I use a Google Calendar and texts. It's fine.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** But wouldn't it be easier with a dedicated app that handled payments too?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** When did someone last cancel on you late? What happened to that slot?

**Why better:** "It's fine" might be true. The bad response argues. The good one tests "fine" against a specific recent incident. Either a cost emerges or the founder learns this trainer isn't a customer.
**Key signal to find:** Lost income per week from no-shows, and whether they've ever tried charging a cancellation fee. If they refuse to charge fees for relationship reasons, a deposit product is dead for this segment.
**Next best question:** "Have you ever charged for a late cancel? How did that go?"

---

### Scenario 6 — Marketplace: Used camera gear

**Hypothesis:** Photographers would use a specialized marketplace over eBay for used gear because of trust and condition grading.
**Customer:** Semi-pro wedding photographer who buys and sells gear a few times a year.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** eBay's sketchy but it's where the volume is.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** What if there was a marketplace just for photographers, with verified condition grading? Would you list there?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** Tell me about the last thing you sold. Where, how long did it take, what went wrong?

**Why better:** The bad response pitches and gets a hypothetical yes. The good one gets the real selling process, which will reveal whether "sketchy" ever cost them anything and whether "volume" is the thing they'd never give up.
**Key signal to find:** A specific bad transaction with a dollar loss, and whether they've used any alternative (forums, local shops, specialty sites). Marketplace startups die on liquidity; "where the volume is" is the elephant.
**Next best question:** "Have you ever sold somewhere other than eBay? Why, and how did it go?"

---

### Scenario 7 — AI product: Contract review

**Hypothesis:** In-house counsel at mid-size companies would use AI to first-pass review vendor contracts.
**Customer:** Sole in-house lawyer at a 300-person company.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** I'd be nervous letting AI touch contracts.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** Totally understandable, but our model is trained specifically on commercial contracts and has a 94% accuracy rate. It's really just a first pass.

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** That's the thing I most need to understand. What would it take for you to trust it, and what would happen if it missed something?

**Why better:** The bad response defends. The good one treats the objection as the scary question's answer and digs for the hardness of the constraint and the consequence of failure. That determines whether the product is possible at all for this buyer.
**Key signal to find:** How they review contracts now (hours, outside counsel spend), and whether "nervous" is a hard no or a "show me." Outside counsel spend is the value ceiling.
**Next best question:** "How many contracts crossed your desk last month, and how many went to outside counsel?"

---

### Scenario 8 — Developer tool: Flaky test detection

**Hypothesis:** Engineering teams with large CI suites lose developer hours to flaky tests and would pay for automated detection and quarantine.
**Customer:** Staff engineer at a 60-engineer company.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** Flaky tests? Oh, we have a whole Slack channel dedicated to complaining about them.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** Ha, classic. So we're building a tool that auto-detects flakes and quarantines them. Do you think your team would adopt it?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** What happened the last time a flake blocked someone?

**Why better:** A Slack channel is a workaround signal, which is good, but "would your team adopt it" is a hypothetical about others. The good response gets an incident and its cost.
**Key signal to find:** Hours lost per week across the team, plus whether anyone's already built an internal retry/quarantine script. If they have, that's both validation and competition.
**Next best question:** "Has anyone on the team built anything to deal with it? What does it do?"

---

### Scenario 9 — Local business: Restaurant inventory

**Hypothesis:** Independent restaurants over-order and waste food, and would pay for a simple inventory tracker.
**Customer:** Owner-operator of a 50-seat neighborhood restaurant.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** Inventory? I eyeball it. Twenty years, I know what I need.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** But studies show restaurants waste up to 10% of food costs. Wouldn't you want to know exactly where that's going?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** When did eyeballing last get it wrong? What did you toss?

**Why better:** The bad response argues with a statistic and asks a leading question. The good one respects the expertise and tests it against an instance. Either a real loss appears, or this owner isn't a customer and that's fine.
**Key signal to find:** A specific spoilage event with a dollar figure, and any prior attempt (a clipboard system, a POS module they turned off). "I eyeball it" plus no losses means no problem.
**Next best question:** "What's your food cost percentage, and has it moved in the last year?"

---

### Scenario 10 — Creator economy: Sponsorship management

**Hypothesis:** Mid-size YouTube creators (100k–500k subs) lose sponsorship revenue to disorganized deal tracking and would pay for a CRM.
**Customer:** Creator with 220k subscribers, does 2–4 sponsored videos a month.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** I manage it all in Notion. Kind of a mess but it works.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** Notion's not really built for that though. Our tool would track deliverables, payments, and contracts in one place. Interested?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** What's the "kind of a mess" part? When did it last cause a problem?

**Why better:** The bad response disparages their current solution and pitches. The good one digs into the gap between "mess" and "works," which is where the value is (or isn't).
**Key signal to find:** A missed deliverable, a late payment they didn't chase, or a deal they forgot. Any of those has a dollar figure. Also: whether they've hired a manager or agency, which is the real competitor and sets the price ceiling.
**Next best question:** "Have you ever missed a payment or deliverable because of tracking? What did it cost?"

---

### Scenario 11 — Fintech: Expense management for nonprofits

**Hypothesis:** Small nonprofits struggle with grant-restricted expense tracking and would pay for a tool that tags spending by grant.
**Customer:** Executive director of a 12-person nonprofit with six active grants.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** Grant reporting is a nightmare. I spend the last week of every quarter on it.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** We can automate that. What if every expense was tagged to a grant at the point of purchase?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** Walk me through last quarter's reporting week. What did you actually do each day?

**Why better:** There's already a strong signal (a week per quarter). The bad response jumps to solution before knowing the workflow. The good one maps the process, which will show where the time actually goes. It may not be tagging.
**Key signal to find:** Where the hours go (reconciliation? narrative writing? chasing receipts?), and whether they've paid a bookkeeper or consultant for this. A funder's compliance requirement is the urgency driver.
**Next best question:** "Has a funder ever pushed back on a report? What happened?"

---

### Scenario 12 — Hardware/IoT: Cold-chain monitoring

**Hypothesis:** Small food distributors lose product to temperature excursions and would pay for cheap sensor monitoring.
**Customer:** Operations manager at a regional seafood distributor with 8 trucks.
**Conversation start:**
> **C:** We have temperature loggers already. They're fine.

**Bad founder response:**
> **F:** Ours are real-time though, with alerts. Yours are just after-the-fact, right? Wouldn't real-time be better?

**Good founder response:**
> **F:** When did you last lose a load, and when did you find out?

**Why better:** The bad response attacks the current solution and leads. The good one tests whether "fine" survives contact with an incident. The gap between "lost" and "found out" is exactly where real-time would matter.
**Key signal to find:** Dollar value of the last loss, and time-to-detection. Also: whether a customer or regulator has ever demanded proof of temperature history. If losses are rare and detection is fast, "fine" is true.
**Next best question:** "What did that load cost, and did the customer find out before you did?"

# Part 14 — Conversation Scorecard

**[Synthesis]** Score yourself within an hour of each conversation. Be harsh; the point is to find your weak category.

| # | Category | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Talk ratio** | I talked more than half the time | Roughly even | They talked 70%+ |
| 2 | **Pitching** | I pitched early or throughout | I pitched, but after discovery | I didn't pitch, or flipped only after every discovery box was filled |
| 3 | **Behavior questions** | Mostly opinions and hypotheticals | Mixed | Every question was about their past or present behavior |
| 4 | **Leading** | I named the problem and they agreed | Some leading | They named problems unprompted; I never suggested answers |
| 5 | **Current solutions** | Didn't ask | Know what they use | Know what they use, what it costs, what's wrong with it, why they haven't switched |
| 6 | **Costs** | No figures | One figure (time or money) | Time, money, and consequence figures |
| 7 | **Frequency** | "Often" | A rough number | A number I could verify, plus the last three instances |
| 8 | **Urgency** | Didn't ask | Asked, vague answer | Know the trigger, the deadline, or the absence of both |
| 9 | **Specific examples** | Zero incidents | One incident | Two or more dated incidents with detail |
| 10 | **What they've tried** | Didn't ask | Know one thing | Know the history of attempts and why each failed |
| 11 | **Facts vs. opinions** | Notes are a blur | Some separation | Two columns, and I could defend every fact with a quote |
| 12 | **Surprise** | Nothing new | One minor surprise | Something that changed an assumption |
| 13 | **Scary question** | Avoided it | Asked it late or softly | Asked it early and got a real answer |
| 14 | **Compliments and fluff** | Logged them as positive | Noticed, didn't redirect | Deflected and anchored every one |
| 15 | **Next step** | "Keep in touch" | A vague follow-up | A commitment (time/intro/money) with a date, or a clear no, plus at least one new name |

**Total: out of 75.**

| Score | Interpretation | Do this |
|---|---|---|
| **15–30** | The conversation produced no evidence, whatever it felt like. Probably pitched and got compliments. | Re-read Chapters 1–2. Level 1 exercises. |
| **31–45** | Some real facts, but leading questions or missing cost/frequency data mean you can't act on it. | Drill anchoring and cost questions. Level 2. |
| **46–60** | Solid discovery. You know their behavior and costs. Probably weak on urgency, scary question, or next step. | Focus on the two lowest categories. Level 3. |
| **61–70** | Expert conversation. Evidence you can build on. | Check: did the evidence change anything? If not, are you still surprised by anyone? |
| **71–75** | Rare. Verify you're not grading yourself generously. | Have a teammate score the same conversation from your notes. |

**Trend rule:** Track scores over ten conversations. A category that's consistently your lowest is your training focus. A total that's flat for ten conversations means you've plateaued and need a partner to review recordings.
